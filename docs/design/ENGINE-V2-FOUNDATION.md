# Race engine v2 — first isolated foundation

Status: **development prototype, not wired to the public race cycle**. The existing `recovery-one-day-4-orders` engine, stored race outputs, database schema and live site remain unchanged. This work starts a separate, versioned engine on top of the latest recovery code so the working cycle can remain the comparison baseline while the replacement is built and balanced.

## Product contract

- Managers commit their lineup, captain, optional road captain, preset, detailed phases and contingencies **before the deadline**. The server snapshots that exact input. The complete race is calculated once after the deadline, and everyone views the same recorded replay. No live manager action is required.
- Men and women have separate races and rankings. They can share stat names and tuning architecture, but balance must be measured separately.
- A simple preset must remain playable; expert orders at 10 km markers or authored route keypoints add depth. Repeated attacks and constant pursuit have energy costs. Several attacking teams can overwhelm one chasing team.
- Leadership affects how a road captain recognises a tactical threat and coordinates a response. It does not directly multiply the captain's physical speed. Future contingency rules may let a road captain switch to a preselected backup leader; a manager does not improvise after the deadline.
- Every kilometre carries elevation, gradient, terrain, surface and related weather. Explicit surface segments are required; a generic `COBBLES` race tag is not enough to invent precise cobbled kilometres.

## Current prototype

`lib/engine/v2/route.mjs` validates a stage and derives one deterministic environmental record per kilometre, including explicitly authored surface and wind-exposure segments. `orders.mjs` normalises simple presets, expert phase overrides and an optional precommitted backup-leader contingency. `tactics.mjs` models attacks versus finite chase capacity, including energy costs and a road-captain leadership response. `physiology.mjs` gives the proposed fourteen sporting skills specific terrain, effort and weather situations; five new skills are derived in memory for legacy riders, not written to the database. `tour.mjs` connects these into an immutable tactical trace; a stronger road captain notices an exhausted captain and executes the selected backup plan sooner. `groups.mjs` tracks per-rider deficits and provisional peloton, breakaway or dropped status. `finish.mjs` turns the final state into a deterministic **experimental** finish order. `tuning.mjs` keeps balance constants in one place. The group and finish calculations do **not** yet represent complete drafting, road positioning, crashes, true rider-by-rider time dynamics, stage standings or a production replay.

The trace records the actual riders ahead, limits one team's active breakaway contingent, prevents a rider from re-attacking while already ahead, and charges continuing energy cost for riding in the break. These are provisional race rules in the isolated model; they must be calibrated against complete group and finish simulations before release.

An attempted attack now has a separate admission event. If the peloton's chase overpowers the new move, that rider pays the attack cost but cannot appear in an older break that still has a positive gap. Race Lab and the recorded frame show attempted attacks separately from riders who joined; the frame also retains attack and chase pressure so replay validation can check that distinction. This changes tactical outcomes and is balance version `v2-prototype-12`.

When a chase closes the break's gap, the frame records which riders were caught. A later attack can establish another break under its precommitted orders, with the earlier riders' spent energy preserved. Replay validation checks the catch against the preceding kilometre.

The break's gap now also changes from its own sustained solo pace relative to the attached bunch, even when no new attack or chase order fires. Terrain, weather, rider ability, current energy and a small bounded cooperation/drafting allowance affect this passive change. The contribution is saved in each replay frame and shown in Race Lab. This first approximation is balance version `v2-prototype-13`; it still uses one shared breakaway gap and should not be read as a complete multi-group road model. A fixed-cast 100-seed run still produced all-or-nothing breakaway outcomes for some presets, so this is a mechanic correction, not completed balance.

For provisional dropped groups, the reference pace comes from riders still attached to the bunch. Riders already distanced cannot lower that reference when many fall behind together. This changed group outcomes in balance version `v2-prototype-11`; it still needs broad seeded calibration before release.

Effort now changes immediate attack and chase pressure as well as the continuing energy bill. The stronger `hard` setting can create or close a gap sooner, but leaves less energy for later kilometres. The multiplier is a central balancing parameter, not a public percentage shown to players.

Selective chase now remembers a detected break while it remains ahead. The road captain's leadership still affects the initial recognition; the team does not abandon an already committed pursuit merely because it has reduced the gap below the original trigger threshold. This pursuit state is part of the deterministic simulation trace, not a mid-race user input.

The `protect` preset now commits saved helpers to a progressively stronger sprint-train chase in the final ten kilometres, paying a matching energy surcharge. This gives a fresh sprint team a real counter to late attacks without making every late break automatically fail. Its balance still needs broad scenario checks before release.

A manager can also precommit `chase_if_threatened` as a break response. If an opposing break reaches the centrally tuned threat gap, the road captain recognises it after a leadership-dependent delay and switches the team's chase rule to `all`; the helpers pay the normal pursuit cost. When the gap closes, the team resumes its scheduled order. The recorded decisions identify the switch and the return, while each frame stores which responses are active. The default remains `hold_plan`, and no post-deadline input is used. This is balance version `v2-prototype-14`. The option is testable in Race Lab but is not yet wired to live manager orders.

A new attack cannot instantly join an existing break that is beyond the tuned bridging reach. The attempt still costs energy, but neither teleports the rider forward nor increases the distant break's gap. The failed bridge is recorded and shown in Race Lab. This is balance version `v2-prototype-15`. The prototype still tracks only one break group; secondary attack groups and a sustained bridge chase need a later group-model expansion.

The provisional finish now preserves the final group's road order: an uncaught break remains ahead of the bunch by at least its recorded gap, and a rider still classified as dropped finishes behind the attached bunch. The final sprint still sorts riders within each group. This removes a contradiction between the last replay frame and its result table in balance version `v2-prototype-16`; full per-rider finishing dynamics remain future work.

The 20-sample varied-roster diagnostic immediately exposed a balance problem rather than proving the model ready: on its flat route, breakaway winners rose to 80% under `protect` and 100% under both `aggressive` and `balanced`. The previous finish calculation had masked surviving breaks by letting bunch riders overtake them without a recorded catch. Catch and finale dynamics need further work before any release; these diagnostic rates are not game targets.

Balance version `v2-prototype-17` introduced a recorded last-metre catch event. It removed the false result in which a bunch sprinter overtook a surviving break with no catch recorded. In the 20-sample flat diagnostic, breakaway winner rates returned to 40% (`protect`), 75% (`aggressive`) and 70% (`balanced`), with finish-line catches recorded in 40%, 25% and 30% of those races. The median pre-finale gaps were under one second, so these results are especially sensitive to finish tuning. That version still caught the entire break as one unit.

Balance version `v2-prototype-18` resolves a caught breakaway rider individually during the finishing burst. A faster teammate may still stay in front; the final frame records the caught rider, the reduced group and its remaining gap. This removes the all-or-nothing catch rule from the prior prototype. The model still has only one road break group before the finish, so sustained secondary bridge groups remain future work.

Balance version `v2-prototype-19` adds a precommitted `breakWork` order (`cooperate` or `sit_on`) at baseline or a valid phase marker. Only riders already in the tracked break who choose to cooperate count towards its shared pace and cooperation bonus; they pay more energy per kilometre. Sitting riders pay a smaller break cost, and a break with no willing worker loses pace. The recorded kilometre lists the riders who actually took pulls, and Race Lab exposes the choice for Amber. Teams with a rider in the tracked break already withhold their helpers from chasing it. This remains a first model of cooperation within one tracked break, not a full account of team loyalty or separate chase groups. Different objectives across multiple road groups still need explicit group and team-objective rules.

Balance version `v2-prototype-20` lets selectively chasing teams tolerate a small gap during the opening quarter of a route. The allowance depends on the remaining distance; a fresh attack, an explicit all-out chase or an activated road-captain response still gets an immediate reaction. A team that waits spends no chase energy on that kilometre, and the choice is recorded for replay. The initial, longer waiting window caused excessive breakaway wins in the varied-roster diagnostic, so the window was reduced before committing. The 20-sample diagnostic then matched the previous version's breakaway win rates across flat, rolling and mountain routes. These are small diagnostic samples, not proof that real race timing or overall balance is solved.

Balance version `v2-prototype-21` gives a rider brought back from the break five subsequent kilometres before they may launch another attack. A preselected rider blocked by this rule appears in the recorded attack events as `recently_caught`; a team without a named attacker may send a fresh teammate instead. This prevents the same rider from immediately recycling a failed break while preserving the possibility of a new one. It does not yet model a separate chase group or attacks launched from inside the break: both require multiple independent road positions and gaps in the core model.

Schema version `v2-prototype-22` makes the active road group the simulator's primary breakaway state. Its `road-N` identity remains stable while that break survives; a catch closes it, and a later break receives the next identity. Each recorded kilometre stores this group, while the older single-break fields remain projections for existing laboratory views and result calculations. Replay validation rejects a mismatched projection or a group identity that changes without a catch. This is an architectural step towards several independent road groups; this version still simulates at most one and does not yet permit an attack from inside it. The 20-sample varied-roster diagnostics matched version 21 on flat, rolling and mountain routes after the refactor.

Balance version `v2-prototype-23` adds a precommitted `captainSupport` order (`hold_position` by default or `drop_back_if_dropped`) at baseline or a valid phase marker. Once the active leader is in the dropped group, an available helper may leave the peloton, spend extra energy and reduce that leader's deficit by a bounded amount each kilometre. A helper already alongside the captain may continue; one who falls too far behind cannot contribute from a distance. Assigned support riders cannot simultaneously attack or chase. The replay records the helper, leader and time recovered; Race Lab can display these events. This is a provisional captain-support rule, not a complete multi-group domestique model. It does not change default order outcomes until a manager selects it.

Balance version `v2-prototype-24` tracks each rider's accumulated attack load separately from their ordinary energy. An attempted move adds load, quiet kilometres gradually reduce it, and high `repeatability` protects the pressure of later attacks. A named attacker remains the manager's chosen rider even when tired; without a named rider, a fresher teammate may be selected. Recorded kilometres mark attacks that have lost sharpness after earlier efforts, without exposing the hidden balance coefficients in Race Lab. In the 20-sample varied-roster diagnostic, breakaway winner rates shifted from 50/75/70% to 45/70/75% on flat routes (`protect`/`aggressive`/`balanced`), 55/80/75% to 45/70/75% on rolling routes, and 60/60/80% to 50/60/65% on mountain routes. These are diagnostic samples, not target rates or proof of balance.

Version `v2-prototype-25` records a finish-line catch even if the caught rider launched and joined a new break on the final kilometre. Previously that simultaneous join and catch could leave the result table showing a rider still ahead despite the finishing sprint catching them. Replay validation now permits a same-kilometre join and catch while requiring every rider in a fully caught group to appear in the catch event. This does not yet model a separate attacking group from an existing break.

Version `v2-prototype-26` connects a planned attack **from** a break to the complete isolated kilometre tour. An expert `breakAttackRiderId` phase makes one attempt on the first kilometre after its valid 10 km or route-keypoint marker. If the named rider is still in a multi-rider break and has enough energy, the attack can create a second road group. Rival riders willing to cooperate can chase the move at an energy cost; teammates do not chase their own rider. The two groups carry stable IDs and separate absolute gaps to the peloton, and per-kilometre rider ability, energy and cooperation can move them apart or bring them back together. A peloton catch can also remove the pursuing group while the leader survives. An unexecuted plan is recorded with a reason.

The offline paired diagnostic `node scripts/engine-v2-break-split.mjs 20 40` compares the same seeds with and without Amber's planned split. At the 40 km marker, 19 of 20 attempts split, and all 19 attackers were caught within five kilometres; the split changed seven winners. Moving that same plan to 80 km caused 17 splits and 17 later merges across 20 seeds, while the 120 km marker found no eligible attack in this fixed cast. These outcomes show that timing matters but also that splits are currently too easy to trigger in this fixture. They are calibration warnings, not balance targets. The existing varied-roster ensemble does not include this order yet.

The saved trace records both groups, the rear group's separate peloton gap, split and merge events, riders caught, and energy costs. Replay validation checks group membership, IDs, order and finish gaps. If two groups survive to the line, `provisionalRoadGroupFinish` preserves their recorded road order; a final-metre sprint catch **between two surviving break groups** is not yet modelled. The normal one-group finishing sprint remains unchanged. Race Lab can precommit Amber's captain to attack after kilometre 40, 80 or 120 and inspect the recorded groups. This is a bounded two-group prototype, not a complete many-group road model: only one split may be active, simultaneous split plans are resolved deterministically with other plans recorded as unexecuted, and group pace and two-group finishing still need broader balance work. It remains separate from production races.

Version `v2-prototype-27` also lets a successful peloton attack form the **rear** of two road groups when the original break is too far away to bridge directly. The new group earns its own gap against the peloton; it never inherits the leader's gap or adds speed to the leader. It can later be caught by the peloton or merge with the group ahead. A recorded `formedChaseGroupId` distinguishes this from an attack within a break, and replay validation requires the new group's identity and riders to match the attack. The two-group limit still applies: a third independent group cannot yet form, and an attempted move that cannot be represented remains a failed bridge. The group dynamics and catch/merge rates still need broad seeded balance work.

Version `v2-prototype-28` removes an artificial near-zero chase-group gap. A move launched too far behind the leading break now earns a gap against the peloton; if that gain covers the leader's remaining gap by the end of the kilometre, the attacker actually joins the leader. Otherwise it forms a distinct chasing group at the gap it earned. The trace names riders who completed the bridge, and replay checks that those riders really joined the original group. This remains a per-kilometre approximation, not a continuous road-position model.

Version `v2-prototype-29` generalises the pure road-group movement and continuity rules to any number of ordered groups. Every group moves by its own computed gap change; groups caught by the peloton leave the road state before surviving neighbours merge, and a chain of overtakes keeps the rearmost surviving group's identity. Tests cover three simultaneous groups, multiple merges and a middle group being caught. The full tour, admission logic, finish and recorded schema **still cap actual races at two groups**. This is a foundation for lifting that cap, not a three-group race release.

Version `v2-prototype-30` lifts the isolated full-tour cap to **three** ordered road groups. A later peloton move can form a chasing group behind an existing chase, or a rider in a multi-rider leading group can split it. Adjacent groups move according to their own riders' abilities and orders, and the recording validates formation, membership, catches, merges and finish order for all three. A complete three-group race and replay now have an integration test. This remains a bounded prototype: at most one new group forms per kilometre, additional moves at the three-group cap cannot form a fourth group, and final-metre catches between surviving break groups are not modelled. Peloton tactics still use the rearmost gap and aggregate riders ahead; independent motives and work in each chase group require further modelling and balance tests.

Version `v2-prototype-31` calculates the rear road group's passive gap to the peloton from **that group's riders alone**. A strong leading group can no longer lend its work to a separate chasing group's gap. Cooperative riders in every road group still pay for their pulls, and the relative pace between adjacent groups still uses their own workers. This corrects an important cross-group speed leak; group-specific team objectives and chase decisions remain future work.

Version `v2-prototype-32` gives each road group a basic team-loyalty participation rule. A rider whose teammate is in any group further up the road sits on rather than taking pulls against them, even when their general order says to cooperate. Other teams' willing workers still pull, and only actual workers pay the extra breakaway work cost. The group-speed calculation and the recorded pull list use the same decision. This is an initial rule, not yet the full conditional team-objective model described in `ENGINE-V2-TEAM-DECISIONS.md`.

Version `v2-prototype-34` allows a precommitted attack from any road group with at least two riders. The attacker can establish a new group between the source and the group ahead, or bridge directly into that group if the earned gap covers the distance. A new split still respects the three-group prototype cap, while a direct bridge does not add a group. Both transitions are recorded and validated for replay; a solo rider cannot split again.

Version `v2-prototype-35` raises the shared full-tour and replay limit to six ordered road groups so successive independent moves need not be rejected at three. The same one-new-group-per-kilometre rule, adjacent-group movement, catch/merge transitions and result ordering apply. Six is an interim simulation bound, not a game rule; group-specific tactics and final-metre interaction still need calibration before a larger field can be trusted.

Version `v2-prototype-36` carries team loyalty into the immediate response to an attack from a chasing group. A rider whose teammate is already in a group ahead no longer spends energy pursuing another rider's move from their group. Other willing defenders still respond, so this changes the attack's earned gap without making it free or guaranteed. It remains a local tactical rule rather than a full assessment of team objectives or overall classification.

Version `v2-prototype-37` resolves simultaneous precommitted attacks by the gap each attack earns against its own group, with rider ID used only for an exact tie. This removes the alphabetical team-ID advantage in the current one-successful-group-move-per-kilometre approximation. A direct bridge to the group ahead remains possible even when six groups already exist; a seventh split does not.

Outside a break, available helpers on `protect` can also shelter their active leader. The leader saves some baseline energy while those helpers spend extra energy; helpers selected for chase work in the same kilometre cannot provide shelter. Positioning, strength, handling and wind skills influence which helpers are effective. This is a first draft of team protection, not a complete drafting or road-position model.

A rider following `conserve` can now recover a little energy after several uninterrupted quiet kilometres in the bunch on calm, flat roads. Higher endurance improves that recovery. Attacking, chasing, sheltering a leader, riding ahead, hard effort, rough terrain and difficult weather interrupt the rest streak. Recovery cannot exceed the rider's starting race capacity after pre-race fatigue; it is not a free refill to 100.

Tactical attack and chase pressure now uses the same kilometre environment as rider movement. Cobbles, gravel, rain, exposed wind and temperature can favour different specialists, rather than changing only the displayed pace. Descending uses the explicit descending skill, and extreme heat or cold lowers ability and raises energy cost with endurance providing partial resilience. These are centrally tuned prototype relationships, not published percentages for players.

Attack candidates and chase helpers now need enough remaining energy, and riders marked dropped cannot contribute to the peloton's chase or launch a new front-group attack. An exhausted helper therefore cannot keep producing chase power after its energy has reached zero; another available rider may take its place. This makes finite team work an actual limit rather than only a cost on paper.

Managers may optionally name a specific attacker in their baseline order or change that choice at a 10 km marker or authored keypoint. `null` returns to automatic selection. A named rider is not silently replaced when already ahead, dropped or exhausted; the recorded trace keeps a reason for the blocked attempt. Simple presets still choose an available rider automatically.

Inputs fail closed on unknown order fields, bad rider IDs, invalid stats, mixed race categories and missing seeds. Team and rider inputs are sorted before calculation, so database row order does not change the saved trace. This is necessary for reproducible results and later replay verification.

The existing feature-gated Race Lab now has a separate **Run kilometre prototype** action. It adapts the same fictional four-team cast to v2 and shows the saved kilometre trace and provisional finish order alongside the older flat-road experiment. Neither calculation reads player accounts or writes database rows; the two models stay visibly labelled so their outputs are not mistaken for live race results.

`recording.mjs` validates every saved kilometre against the final rider list, verifies that breakaway membership follows the previous frame and that team/chase events name actual entrants, and checks that provisional placings agree with the last frame. The recording includes the race category derived from its validated, single-category entrants, the race seed, the normalised locked weather and the canonical committed lineup, all fourteen effective sporting skills, form, fatigue, leadership and pre-race orders for every team. Calculations use those canonical rider values, including derived defaults, so the input snapshot matches what the motor actually read. Replay validation checks that the committed lineup matches the recorded result roster and category. Men and women remain separate race outputs; the prototype uses the same physical coefficients for both until category-specific balancing has evidence. Playback reads a copy of an existing frame; it has no simulator dependency and does not rerun the race when the viewer seeks. This is an in-memory contract only. Server-side storage, source-roster verification, integrity signatures, access control and production replay are still outstanding. A stored category label alone cannot prove that a later writer did not alter it.
Replay validation also checks that the first bunch finisher is at least the final recorded group gap behind the last surviving breakaway rider (within result rounding). This catches a replay whose finale table contradicts its final road state; it does not replace server-side integrity checks.

All input, event seed, route version, locked weather, order snapshot, tuning version and engine version must be stored with a future committed race. A replay should be rendered from stored simulation events, never rerun with current tuning after a balance change. New sporting stats must get explicit migration/default rules for existing riders; potential caps must remain server-owned and hidden.

## Next gates

The team-intent, captain-support and rider-autonomy rules to implement after independent road groups are defined in [ENGINE-V2-TEAM-DECISIONS.md](ENGINE-V2-TEAM-DECISIONS.md).

For repeatable balance diagnostics, run `node scripts/engine-v2-balance.mjs 100`. It compares the four plans using the same 100 seeds and reports wins, placings, energy, breakaways, dropped riders, attempted attacks and successful admissions. This is a fixed fictional flat-road cast; its rates are diagnostic, not a target distribution for the full game.

Run `node scripts/engine-v2-ensemble.mjs 20` for a second, paired diagnostic across flat, rolling and mountain routes, varied fictional specialists, changing weather and both race categories. Each strategy within a sample uses the same roster and conditions. These metrics reveal balance failures that the fixed cast cannot expose; no player data or live services are read. The report separates photo-finish break wins (winner under one second ahead of the first bunch finisher) from clear wins (at least five seconds) and reports the median winning margin among break wins. In a 20-sample men's run on balance version 24, 45–75% of races across the three routes and plans had a break winner, but 40–70 percentage points were photo finishes and at most five percentage points were clear wins. Thus the headline break-win rate mostly describes tight finishes, not runaway breaks. This small sample is not a balance target, and the final recorded group gap measures the **last** surviving break rider relative to the bunch, not the winner's margin.

1. Replace the initial group/finish approximation with per-rider sustained pace, drafting, terrain/surface/weather effects, energy recovery, road positioning and realistic group formation. Write scenario tests in which each visible stat changes the action it is meant to govern.
2. Expand precommitted contingencies beyond the first exhausted-captain rule, ensuring each has observable triggers, response delays and failure reasons. Balance leadership without making the captain's own speed depend on it.
3. Produce complete deterministic results and a recorded replay, then compare thousands of seeded races against the existing engine. Check role diversity, order impact, sensible energy use, upset rates and men/women separately. Tune constants centrally without rewriting the simulation.
4. Only after the new engine passes these gates: design a reversible database migration and isolated preview release. Do not change production data, the live race runner or scheduled jobs as part of this foundation.
